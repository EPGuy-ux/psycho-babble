# Early number: a progression and assessment guide for schools

North Somerset Educational Psychology Service · Draft · Nov 6, 2026 · @JJ Johnson

## How to use this guide

Use this guide to find where a child's early number understanding has broken down, and to decide what to teach next. It is for teachers, SENCos and teaching assistants working with children from Nursery to Year 3. You can also use it with older pupils with SEND who are still building early number.

The guide has five parts. Part 1 sets out 15 steps in early number, with what you see when a child is secure and the errors you are likely to meet. Part 2 gives you short tasks to check each step. Part 3 suggests teaching activities for each step. Parts 4 and 5 help you choose a programme and run it well.

Children do not move through the steps in a fixed line. A child may recognise three dots at a glance before they can count a set of five. Treat the steps as a map. When you assess, check the steps either side of the one you are interested in.

Start teaching at the earliest step the child cannot yet do with confidence. If you teach a later step first, the child learns a procedure they cannot use outside the lesson where they learnt it.

## Why early number matters

Children who start school with secure early number skills tend to stay ahead. In a study of six large data sets, Duncan and colleagues (2007) found that maths skills at school entry predicted later attainment more strongly than early reading or attention. Low numeracy in adulthood links to unemployment and poorer health (Bynner and Parsons, 2006).

The Education Endowment Foundation's Early Years Toolkit estimates that early numeracy approaches add around seven months' progress on average. The EEF rates the strength of this evidence as limited, and the figure is an average across many approaches. It does not tell you what one programme will achieve.

### Common misconceptions

These beliefs come up often in schools. Each one can lead you to teach the wrong thing.

*"She can count to 20, so she knows her numbers to 20."* Saying the number words in order is step 2. A child can recite to 20 and still grab a handful when you ask for five. Use the "give me" task to check steps 4 and 5.

*"He needs more practice."* If a child does not yet understand the idea underneath, more practice builds a routine they cannot use in a new problem. Staff may then put the difficulty down to poor memory. Go back to the step where the child's understanding breaks down, and teach from there.

*"Cubes and counters are for the younger children."* Use objects at the step the child is working at, whatever their age. Choose materials that match the idea you are teaching. A number track with equal squares shows children the number line better than a pile of counters (Laski and Siegler, 2014).

*"She'll catch up when she's ready."* Children with maths difficulties move through the same steps as their peers, later (Siegler and Braithwaite, 2017). Teaching moves them on. If you wait, the gap stays.

*"He knows his number bonds. He answers in a flash."* Fast answers learnt by rote can fall apart when the numbers change. Ask the child how they know. A child who explains 6 + 7 from 6 + 6 has an understanding they can build on.

## Part 1: the early number progression

The 15 steps run from comparing groups by eye to early multiplication and division. Steps 1 to 5 build the meaning of number. Steps 6 to 9 build the links between small numbers that children need for calculation. Steps 10 to 15 extend those links to numbers to 100 and to solving problems.

### Step 1: Comparing and matching

**When it is secure.** The child can say which of two groups has more when the difference is clear, such as 3 and 8. They can give one cup to each teddy. They can put five towers of 1 to 5 cubes in order of height.

**Common errors.** The child picks the longer row as "more" whatever the number of items. They leave items out or double up when matching.

### Step 2: Saying the counting sequence

**When it is secure.** The child says the number words in the same order each time, first to 10 and then to 20. They can start from a number other than one. They can tell you the number after 6 without going back to one. They can count back from 10.

**Common errors.** The child skips or repeats words, such as "one, two, four, five". They need to start at one to find the next number. They confuse the teens, such as "eleventeen".

### Step 3: Subitising

Subitising means seeing how many are in a small group without counting.

**When it is secure.** The child sees one, two or three objects and says how many at once. Later they recognise up to five, and familiar patterns such as dice dots and ten-frame rows.

**Common errors.** The child counts one by one even for two items. They give the same number word, such as "two", for every small group.

### Step 4: Counting a set

Gelman and Gallistel (1978) describe the principles a child needs to count a set. The child counts each object once. They say the number words in a fixed order. They understand that the order of counting makes no difference to the total, and that you can count any kind of object.

**When it is secure.** The child touches or moves each object once while saying one number word. They get the same total when they count from the other end. They can count a scattered set and a set of mixed objects.

**Common errors.** The child says more words than they touch, or touches more objects than they say words. They count an object twice, or lose track of which objects they have counted in a scattered set.

### Step 5: Knowing how many (cardinality)

**When it is secure.** After counting, the child gives the last number they said as the answer to "How many?" without counting again. They can give you a set number of objects from a larger pile. They know the number stays the same when you spread the objects out or push them together.

Children learn this one number at a time. They learn what "one" means, then "two", then "three" and "four", before they grasp the rule for all numbers (Le Corre and Carey, 2007). A child who counts a row of objects with ease may still not understand that the last number tells you how many (Nunes and Bryant, 2009). The "give me" task is the clearest test.

**Common errors.** The child counts again when you ask "How many?" They grab a handful when you ask for five. They say a spread-out row has more.

### Step 6: Linking numerals to words and quantities

**When it is secure.** The child reads the numerals 0 to 10. They match each numeral to its number word and to a set of objects. They can put numeral cards in order and show a numeral on their fingers.

**Common errors.** The child names a numeral but cannot make a set of that size. They put cards in order by rote and cannot find a missing card.

### Step 7: Comparing numbers and the number line

**When it is secure.** The child says which of two numbers is bigger without counting objects. They place numbers on a line marked 0 and 10 with roughly even spaces. They use the words more, fewer, bigger and smaller with accuracy.

Siegler and Braithwaite (2017) show that young children first squash the larger numbers together at the end of a number line. Most children space 0 to 10 evenly by age 5 or 6, and 0 to 100 by age 7 or 8. Children with maths difficulties go through the same change, later.

**Common errors.** The child bunches 6, 7, 8 and 9 near 10. They judge which number is bigger by counting up from one each time.

### Step 8: Part-whole understanding

**When it is secure.** The child knows that a number is made of smaller numbers: 5 is 4 and 1, and also 3 and 2. They find the partners of 5, and then of 10, first with objects and fingers and then without. They see a ten-frame showing 7 as 5 and 2.

Part-whole understanding underpins later work on tens and ones.

**Common errors.** The child counts both parts from one to find the total. They do not see that 3 and 2 makes the same total as 2 and 3.

### Step 9: Early addition and subtraction

**When it is secure.** The child solves simple stories with objects, such as "You have 4 sweets and I give you 3 more". Their strategies move through four stages:

1. Counting all: counting both sets from one.
2. Counting on: starting from the first number, or the larger one, and counting the rest.
3. Reasoning: working out a new fact from a known one, such as 6 + 7 from 6 + 6.
4. Recall: knowing the answer.

Stages 3 and 4 depend on stage 2. Baroody and colleagues (2009) recommend that you teach number bonds through reasoning about relationships, and keep memorising for later.

**Common errors.** The child counts all from one in Year 2. They include the starting number when counting on, so 5 + 3 becomes "5, 6, 7". They recite facts they have memorised and cannot use them to solve a problem.

### Step 10: Ten as a unit and numbers to 20

**When it is secure.** The child sees 10 as one group as well as ten ones. They know that 14 is 10 and 4, and can make it with a full ten-frame and 4 more. They count on from 10 to find a teen number, without counting the ten from one.

The English teen names hide this structure. "Fourteen" says the 4 first, and "eleven" and "twelve" give no clue to the ten at all. You need to point the structure out.

**Common errors.** The child counts all 14 cubes from one each time. They write fourteen as 41. They confuse teen and decade numbers, such as fifteen and fifty.

### Step 11: Number facts within 20

**When it is secure.** The child works out new facts from facts they know. They use doubles and near doubles: 7 + 8 is 7 + 7 and 1 more. They bridge through ten: 8 + 5 is 8 + 2 to make 10, then 3 more. With practice, they recall many of these facts at speed.

Accuracy and understanding come before speed (Haring and colleagues, 1978). Once a child can work out a fact reliably, short timed practice and number games build fluency. Fluent facts free the child's working memory for solving problems.

**Common errors.** The child counts on in ones for every fact, including 9 + 9. They give fast answers that are often wrong, because they learnt the facts by rote. They know 8 + 2 = 10 and do not use it to solve 8 + 5.

### Step 12: Additive reasoning and word problems

Vergnaud (1997) sorts addition and subtraction problems into types. Children meet three types first:

- **Combine:** *Ali has 3 red pens and 2 blue pens. How many pens altogether?*
- **Change:** *Ali has 3 cars. His gran gives him 2 more. How many does he have now?*
- **Compare:** *Mia has 5 stickers and Tom has 3. How many more does Mia have?*

**When it is secure.** The child solves all three types with numbers to 10, using objects or a drawing. They solve change problems where the start is unknown: *I had some sweets. I ate 2 and now I have 3. How many did I have?* They know that addition and subtraction undo each other: if 3 + 2 = 5, then 5 − 2 = 3.

With the same numbers, a problem with an unknown start or an unknown change is harder than one with an unknown result. Teach all three positions.

**Common errors.** The child picks the operation from a key word, so "more" always means add. They add every number in the problem. They can calculate 5 − 2 but do not recognise it in a comparing story.

### Step 13: Counting in tens and the structure of numbers to 100

**When it is secure.** The child counts to 100 and back, and crosses each ten without stopping: 28, 29, 30. They count in tens from zero and from other numbers, such as 3, 13, 23. They count in twos and fives. They use the pattern of the numbers to 100 to work out the next number, so they do not need to memorise each one.

**Common errors.** The child stalls at a change of ten: "28, 29, twenty-ten". They count in tens from 10 and cannot start from 3. They recite fives by rote and cannot say what comes after 35.

### Step 14: Place value and calculating to 100

**When it is secure.** The child knows that the 4 in 43 means four tens. They make 43 with tens and ones in more than one way, such as 3 tens and 13 ones. They place numbers on a 0 to 100 line with roughly even spaces. They add and subtract 10 without counting in ones, and add two 2-digit numbers by working with the tens and the ones.

Most children space 0 to 100 evenly by age 7 or 8 (Siegler and Braithwaite, 2017). A child who puts 20 near the middle of the line has an unsure sense of how big these numbers are.

**Common errors.** The child reads 43 as "four, three". They count on in ones to add 10. They write forty-three as 403.

### Step 15: Early multiplication and division

**When it is secure.** The child makes equal groups and finds the total: 3 plates with 2 biscuits on each. They share a set fairly between a given number of people. They also put a set into groups of a given size and find how many groups. They double and halve small numbers. They link counting in twos, fives and tens to equal groups.

These problems ask more of the child than additive problems, because they link two kinds of quantity, such as plates and biscuits.

**Common errors.** The child shares unevenly and does not check that the groups match. They add the number of groups to the number in each group, so 3 plates of 2 biscuits becomes 5. They recite times tables and cannot use them in a problem.

## Part 2: assessing early number

You can check most steps in a 10 to 15 minute session with one child, a pile of cubes and a few cards.

### Before you start

- Work one-to-one, somewhere quiet, with real objects the child can touch and move.
- Start one or two steps below where you expect difficulty, so the child begins with success.
- Record how the child reached each answer. A child who counts from one to get the right answer is at a different point from a child who knows it at a glance.
- When a child gets stuck, make the task easier: use smaller numbers, or let them see and handle the objects. When they succeed, make it harder: use larger numbers, or hide the objects. Graves and Stringer (2023) use these adjustments in both assessment and teaching.
- Stop at the first step where the child makes repeated errors. That step is where your teaching starts.
- Repeat the tasks at the end of each block of teaching, around every six weeks.

### Tasks for each step

The words in italics are what you say to the child.

**Step 1: Comparing and matching**

1. Put out a group of 3 counters spread wide and a group of 8 close together. *Which has more?* Note whether the child chooses by the space the group takes up.
2. Give the child five towers of 1 to 5 cubes, mixed up. *Put these in order, from smallest to biggest.*

**Step 2: Saying the counting sequence**

1. *Count for me, as far as you can.* Note the first error.
2. *Start at 4 and keep counting.* Repeat from 7.
3. *What number comes after 6?* Note whether the child goes back to one.
4. *Count back from 10.*

**Step 3: Subitising**

1. Show a card with 2 dots for two seconds, then cover it. *How many?* Repeat with 1, 3, 4 and 5 dots, in a mixed order.
2. Use both dice patterns and random arrangements. Note whether the child counts, or knows at a glance.

**Step 4: Counting a set**

1. Put out 7 cubes in a row. *How many are there?* Watch whether each touch matches one number word.
2. Scatter 7 cubes. Ask again. Note whether the child keeps track of counted cubes, for example by moving them aside.
3. *Count them again, starting from this end.* Note whether the child expects the same total.

**Step 5: Knowing how many**

1. After the child counts a set, cover it. *So how many are there?* Note whether they answer without counting again.
2. Put out a pile of 15 cubes. *Give me 3.* Then ask for 5, then 7. Record the largest number the child gives with accuracy.
3. Make two rows of 6 cubes, side by side, one to one. *Are there more here, more here, or are they the same?* Spread one row out and ask again.

**Step 6: Linking numerals to words and quantities**

1. Show numeral cards 0 to 10 in a mixed order. *What number is this?*
2. *Put these cards in order.* Remove one while the child looks away. *Which one is missing?*
3. Show the card for 6. *Give me this many cubes.* Then: *Show me this many fingers.*

**Step 7: Comparing numbers and the number line**

1. *Which is bigger, 5 or 8?* Repeat with 3 and 9, then 7 and 6. Note whether the child counts up from one.
2. Draw a line with 0 at one end and 10 at the other. *Show me where 7 goes.* Repeat with 2 and 5.

**Step 8: Part-whole understanding**

1. Show 5 cubes. Hide some under a cup. *I had 5. How many are hiding?* Repeat with different amounts.
2. Show a ten-frame with 7 counters. *How many? How did you know?* Note whether the child sees 5 and 2.
3. *Show me 5 on your fingers another way.*

**Step 9: Early addition and subtraction**

1. Tell a story with objects. *You have 4 sweets and I give you 3 more. How many now?*
2. Put 5 cubes under a cloth and 3 beside it. *There are 5 under here and 3 here. How many altogether?* Note whether the child counts on from 5 or wants to lift the cloth.
3. Ask *4 + 4* and then *4 + 5*. Note whether the child uses the first answer to work out the second.
4. After each answer: *How did you work it out?*

**Step 10: Ten as a unit and numbers to 20**

1. Show a full ten-frame and 4 loose counters. *How many?* Note whether the child starts from 10 or counts from one.
2. Give the child a ten-frame and a pile of counters. *Make 16.*
3. *Write fourteen.* Then: *Write forty.*

**Step 11: Number facts within 20**

1. *What is 6 + 6? So what is 6 + 7?* Note whether the child uses the double.
2. *What is 8 + 5? How did you work it out?* Listen for making ten.
3. Ask ten facts within 20 in a mixed order. Record which ones the child knows at once and which they work out.

**Step 12: Additive reasoning and word problems**

Let the child use cubes or draw.

1. *There are 4 red cubes and 3 blue cubes. How many altogether?*
2. *I had some sweets. I ate 2. Now I have 4. How many did I have at the start?*
3. *Ella has 7 stickers. Sam has 4. How many more does Ella have?*
4. *4 + 3 = 7. So what is 7 − 3?* Note whether the child calculates or uses the link.

**Step 13: Counting in tens and the structure of numbers to 100**

1. *Count from 27. Keep going until I say stop.* Stop at 43.
2. *Count in tens, starting at 6.*
3. *Count in fives from zero.* Then: *What comes after 35?*

**Step 14: Place value and calculating to 100**

1. Write 43. *What does the 4 stand for? Show me with these.* Give the child tens sticks and single cubes.
2. Draw a line with 0 at one end and 100 at the other. *Show me where 50 goes.* Repeat with 20 and 85.
3. *What is 10 more than 36? What is 10 less than 52?* Note whether the child counts in ones.
4. *What is 23 + 14? How did you work it out?*

**Step 15: Early multiplication and division**

1. Put out 3 plates. *Put 2 biscuits on each plate. How many biscuits altogether?*
2. Give the child 12 counters. *Share these fairly between 3 teddies. How many does each teddy get?*
3. Give the child 12 counters and some plates. *Put 3 counters on each plate. How many plates do you need?*
4. *What is double 4? What is half of 10?*

### Recording

For each task, note what the child did, the numbers they managed, and the strategy they used. Mark each step as secure, emerging or not yet. Your teaching plan starts at the first step marked emerging or not yet.

## Part 3: teaching each step

Start at the first step you marked emerging or not yet in Part 2. Keep the earlier steps going in short warm-ups so the child does not lose them.

### Principles

- **Plan short, frequent sessions.** In Graves and Stringer's (2023) pilot, children had daily one-to-one sessions of 10 to 20 minutes over ten weeks.
- **Use the same numbers across the session.** Graves and Stringer build each session from counting (2 to 3 minutes), then number concepts, calculation and a word problem (5 to 7 minutes each). If you work on 4 in the concepts part, use 4 in the calculation and the story. The child then sees how the parts connect.
- **Connect objects, pictures and symbols.** Show the child how each one represents the same idea. Keep objects available, and go back to them when the child needs them.
- **Hide objects when the child is ready.** Hide a set once the child can reason about a quantity they already know. Reveal it to check. If the child guesses, use smaller numbers or leave more in view.
- **Model and share the language.** Use *my turn, together, your turn*. Say the words you want the child to use: more, fewer, altogether, the same as, how many.
- **Ask how they know.** *How did you work it out?* tells you more than the answer does. Children can explain by showing, moving, pointing or drawing, as well as by speaking.
- **Play linear board games.** Siegler and Ramani (2008) found that a few short sessions of a simple 1 to 10 race game improved young children's number skills. Use a straight track of numbered squares in a line. Start with a die or spinner showing only 1 and 2.
- **Build speed last.** Once the child is accurate, short, mixed practice builds fluency.

### Activities for each step

**Step 1: Comparing and matching**

- **Enough for everyone?** Give each toy one cup. Set up times when there are too many cups, too few, or exactly enough. Ask: *Does everyone have one? How can we check?*
- **Match to compare.** Start with two collections that differ a lot in number. Pair the objects to check which has more, fewer or the same. Later, set a few large objects against more small ones, so size does not give the answer away.

**Step 2: Saying the counting sequence**

- **Count, pause, continue.** Take turns to say a short counting sequence. Pause after 4 and ask the child to carry on from 5. Change the starting number, and extend the range over time.
- **Count back in a rhyme.** Use a countdown rhyme the child knows, with objects or pictures. Start from different numbers, such as 7 or 3. Pause for the child to say the next number. Counting back puts more load on working memory, so give extra support here.

**Step 3: Subitising**

- **See it, show it.** Show one to three objects for a moment, then cover them. Ask the child to show the same number with fingers or counters. Use familiar and unfamiliar arrangements. Extend to four and five when the child is ready.
- **What did you see?** Show a small dot arrangement. Ask: *How many? What did you notice?* Accept "I just saw three". Later, talk about the parts: *I saw two and two, so four.*

**Step 4: Counting a set**

- **Move and count.** Move each object from an uncounted area into a tray, saying one number word for each move. Progress from a row to scattered objects. Then count pictures, marking each one as you count it.
- **Be the counting teacher.** A puppet counts a set. Some counts are right. In others, the puppet misses an object or counts one twice. Ask the child to check, explain the mistake and show a reliable way to count.

**Step 5: Knowing how many**

- **Collect an order.** Ask for a small number of objects from a larger pile: *Please bring four cars.* Model counting and stopping at four, then sum up: *Four cars altogether.* Take turns to give and check orders.
- **Same number, new arrangement.** Count a set, then spread it out or rearrange it. Ask: *Have we added any or taken any away? How many now?* Check together if the child is unsure.

**Step 6: Linking numerals to words and quantities**

- **Fill the labelled pot.** Label pots with numerals and put the matching number of objects in each. Include zero as an empty pot. Sometimes put in the wrong number for the child to check and correct.
- **Find another way.** Choose a numeral and match it to objects, fingers and dot cards. Then reverse the task: show a quantity and ask the child to find its numeral. Vary the arrangements and the styles of numeral.

**Step 7: Comparing numbers and the number line**

- **Race and count on.** Play on a straight 1 to 10 track with equal squares, using a spinner showing 1 or 2. The child says the numbers on the squares as they move: from 4, a move of two is "5, 6". Children learn more when they count on using the numbers on the board (Laski and Siegler, 2014).
- **Build, then estimate.** Make a 0 to 10 line with equal spaces, and link each position to a quantity. Later, remove most of the labels and keep 0, 5 and 10. Ask: *Where would 7 go? How do you know?*

**Step 8: Part-whole understanding**

- **Split and describe.** Share five counters between two mats. Move one counter at a time to find each split, including five and zero. Say: *Three and two make five.* Swap the mats: *What changed? What stayed the same?*
- **Hide a part.** Agree the total, then cover some counters. Ask: *Five altogether, two showing. How many are hidden?* Reveal and check. Start with a smaller total if the child needs it.

**Step 9: Early addition and subtraction**

- **Act, show, record.** Act out joining and taking-away stories with small numbers. Show each story with counters, then with a drawing and the matching number sentence. Ask: *What changed? What does each number show?*
- **Keep the first number.** Put out five counters, cover them, and add two counters the child can see. Model: *Five… six, seven.* For subtraction, start with seven and take two away. Reveal or rebuild the set to check.

**Step 10: Ten as a unit and numbers to 20**

- **Make and exchange ten.** Count ten loose sticks and bundle them. Swap the bundle for ten loose sticks and back again: *One ten is ten ones.* Add four loose sticks to make fourteen.
- **Build, name, match.** Show fourteen on two ten-frames, with one frame full. Say: *Fourteen is ten and four.* Match it to the numeral 14. Build other teen numbers and compare what stays the same.

**Step 11: Number facts within 20**

- **Use a known fact.** Build 6 + 6, then change it to 6 + 7. Ask: *What changed? What must happen to the answer?* Link the related subtraction facts, such as 13 − 6 = 7.
- **Make ten, then practise.** Show 8 + 5 by splitting the five into two and three. For 13 − 5, take away three to reach ten, then two more. Once the child is accurate, revisit these facts in short, mixed practice.

**Step 12: Additive reasoning and word problems**

- **Same numbers, different stories.** Use 3, 2 and 5 in combine, change and compare stories. Act them out and draw them. For compare stories, line up the two rows side by side and keep both in view, so the child sees how many more.
- **Move the missing number.** Tell linked stories: *Three cars arrive, then two more. Some cars arrive, then two more make five. Three cars arrive, then some more make five.* Ask what you know and what you need to find.

**Step 13: Counting in tens and the structure of numbers to 100**

- **Count what changes.** Start with three loose sticks and add bundles of ten: 3, 13, 23, 33. Ask: *What changes? What stays the same?* Take bundles away to count back.
- **Build the counting pattern.** Count pairs of objects, groups of five and bundles of ten, pointing to each whole group. To practise crossing a ten, build 28, 29 and 30, exchanging ten ones for a ten.

**Step 14: Place value and calculating to 100**

- **Same number, different grouping.** Build 43 as four tens and three ones. Exchange one ten for ten ones to make three tens and thirteen ones. Ask: *Why is it still 43?*
- **Change the tens or the ones.** Build 36, add one ten, and compare 36 with 46. Build 23 + 14 by combining the tens and the ones. Reverse the action for subtraction. Write the matching number sentences beside the objects.
- **Extend the line.** Use the step 7 routine on a 0 to 100 line, with 0, 50 and 100 marked.

**Step 15: Early multiplication and division**

- **Make equal groups.** Put two counters on each of three plates. Say: *Three groups of two.* Find the total. Arrange the six counters in rows and columns, and describe both ways of seeing them. Link two equal groups to doubling.
- **Share or group?** With twelve counters, compare two tasks: share them between three plates, and put three on each plate. Ask: *Are we finding how many on each plate, or how many plates?* Share between two to explore halving.

## Part 4: choosing a programme

You do not need a bought programme to teach early number well. If you choose one, pick it for the step your children need, and check the evidence first. The table shows the EEF evidence as of November 2026.

| [Counting Collections](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/promising-programmes/counting-collections) | Reception | Whole class | Weekly sessions and a classroom counting library; training from the developer | +1 month (efficacy trial) | No additional progress |
| [onebillion](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/research-agenda-themes-priority-areas/research-agenda-theme-early-maths) | Year 1 | Targeted pupils | App-based | +3 months | Two months less progress than other pupils |
| [Mathematical Reasoning](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/promising-programmes/mathematical-reasoning) | Year 2 | Whole class | Replaces one lesson a week for about three months; teacher training | +1 month (2026 effectiveness trial, high security) | +2 months |

An efficacy trial tests a programme in a small number of schools with close support from the developer. An effectiveness trial tests it in many schools under everyday conditions. Give more weight to effectiveness trials.

### A suggested pattern

- **Nursery:** Maths Champions, to strengthen practice across the setting. The Department for Education is funding places in 2026.
- **Reception:** Counting Collections for the class. Disadvantaged children made no extra progress in the trial, so add short adult-led teaching from this guide for any child not secure at steps 3 to 5.
- **Year 1:** No programme yet has strong evidence for all pupils. Plan targeted teaching from this guide. If you use onebillion, track the progress of disadvantaged pupils closely.
- **Year 2:** Mathematical Reasoning for the class, and 1stClass@Number 1 for children about a year behind.
- **Any age, persistent difficulties:** one-to-one teaching from this guide's progression. Involve your link EP.

### Programmes still being tested

The EEF is running effectiveness trials of Reception Jigsaw and of the NCETM's Mastering Number at Reception and Key Stage 1. Both are due to report in summer 2027. Many schools use Mastering Number, and it fits the research on early number. We do not yet know its impact. We will update this guide when the results come out.

### A programme to use with caution

In the first EEF trial of [Catch Up Numeracy](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/catch-up-numeracy-2015), pupils gained 3 months. Pupils who had the same amount of one-to-one time with a teaching assistant, without the programme, gained 4 months. In a second, larger trial, Catch Up Numeracy added nothing over well-planned teaching assistant support, and pupils eligible for free school meals made 2 months less progress. The EEF has removed it from its list of promising projects.

The lesson applies to any programme. Regular, structured time with a skilled adult drives much of the gain.

### Questions to ask before you buy

- Has the EEF, or a similar body, tested it in an effectiveness trial?
- Did disadvantaged pupils gain as much as other pupils?
- Can you deliver it as designed, with the staff time and training it needs?
- Which steps in this guide does it teach, and do your children need those steps?

## Part 5: delivering well

Plan these points before the first session.

### Time and group size

- **Little and often.** Aim for 10 to 20 minutes a day. Plan in blocks of about ten weeks.
- **Match the group to the need.** Teach children with the widest gaps one-to-one. Children about a year behind can work in groups of up to four.
- **Timetable it from September.** The NFER evaluators of Catch Up Numeracy advise you to plan structured interventions into the timetable from the start of the school year. Record any session that does not happen, and the reason.

### Staffing

Teaching assistants can get strong results when you set them up well. The NFER evaluators of 1stClass@Number 1 linked its impact to three features: each topic built on the one before, staff followed a clear plan, and they had good training.

- Give the adult training on the progression in this guide, and time to plan.
- Name a teacher who oversees the teaching and reviews progress with the adult.
- Keep the same adult with the child for the whole block where you can.
- Keep the routine of each session the same, so the child spends no effort working out what comes next.

### Linking to class teaching

- Share the plan with the class teacher, including the step the child is working on.
- Use the same words, materials and models in the session and in class.
- Give the child a chance to use the new skill in class within the same week.

### Using a bought programme

Deliver it as the developer designed it, with the full number of sessions. In the 1stClass@Number 1 trial, results varied a lot between schools. The EEF expects schools that deliver the programme as intended to get results close to the trial's.

### Reviewing progress

Repeat the Part 2 tasks at about six weeks and at the end of each block. Use the same tasks and numbers you used at the start, so you can compare. Then decide:

- **Secure:** move to the next step.
- **Progress, not yet secure:** carry on at the same step for another block.
- **Little or no progress:** check the child is working at the right step, and change the approach.
- **Little or no progress after two blocks:** involve your link EP.

### Measuring impact

Keep a simple record for each child: the step at the start, the step at the end, and the number of sessions planned and delivered. If your school uses a standardised early number test, give it before and after the block. Records across a group of children show you whether the teaching is working, and help you decide where to put staff time next year.

## When to involve your link EP

Talk to your link educational psychologist if:

- the child has had two blocks of targeted teaching at the right step and has made little progress
- the child is still not secure at steps 4 and 5 by the end of Year 1
- the child's profile is uneven, with gaps scattered across many steps
- the child has other needs, such as language, attention or memory difficulties, that make it hard to tell what is getting in the way
- the child is anxious about maths or avoids number work.

Your EP can carry out a fuller diagnostic assessment and help you plan teaching from it. In North Somerset, EPs use the PAC Maths Diagnostic Assessments developed by Dr Frances Graves: Number Foundations for children at the earliest stages, and Numbers to 120 for children beyond them. In a small pilot, children from Reception to Year 4 with high-level SEND made significant progress within ten weeks of teaching planned from these assessments (Graves and Stringer, 2023).

Bring your records from Part 2 to the consultation. They show your EP where to start.
